\begin{abstract}
Automated tracking of morphology and laboratory-mice posture usually
runs instance segmentation and pose estimation as two separate
pipelines, which wastes computation, or uses proposal-driven detectors
that blur object boundaries through quantization. To address these
limitations, we propose \textbf{HybridMTLNet}, a unified, box-free
multi-task learning framework for simultaneous multi-instance
segmentation and keypoint localization in a single forward pass. The
architecture uses a shared backbone encoder, a dense per-pixel
segmentation decoder, and a deconvolutional pose head. During training,
we apply Hungarian matching on mask IoU to assign predictions to
ground-truth instances in an order-invariant manner. We evaluate
HybridMTLNet on 3{,}710 manually annotated images spanning eight mouse
behavioral tests, averaged over three random seeds. Our model achieves
a Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK@0.05) of 0.9605 and a mean
Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 0.8083, matching or surpassing
dedicated single-task baselines. A controlled ablation shows that the
multi-task model nearly matches its single-task counterparts of the
same architecture---preserving segmentation accuracy at only a small
cost in pose---while roughly halving the computation.
\keywords{Multi-Task Learning \and Computer Vision \and Image Understanding \and Instance Segmentation \and Pose Estimation \and Hungarian Matching \and Laboratory Mice \and Mice Behavior Analysis}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automated tracking of morphology and laboratory-mice posture plays an
important role in modern behavioral neuroscience and biomedical
research, where accurate monitoring lets researchers quantify complex
behaviors, evaluate phenotypic variation, and assess pharmacological
interventions. Two computer-vision tasks underpin this analysis:
instance segmentation, which outlines each animal's body, and pose
estimation, which locates its joints. In practice, however, these
usually run as two separate pipelines, which wastes computation, or
rely on proposal-driven detectors that blur object boundaries through
quantization.

To address these limitations, we propose HybridMTLNet, a unified,
box-free multi-task learning framework for simultaneous multi-instance
segmentation and keypoint localization in a single forward pass. The
architecture uses a shared backbone encoder, a dense per-pixel
segmentation decoder, and a deconvolutional pose head. During training,
we apply Hungarian matching on mask IoU to assign predictions to
ground-truth instances in an order-invariant manner, removing the need
for bounding-box proposals. Our goal is not a multi-task accuracy
gain---which our ablation does not find---but a single network that
preserves segmentation accuracy at only a small cost in pose while
roughly halving the computation.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unified box-free architecture.} A single network that
        performs multi-instance segmentation and keypoint localization
        in one forward pass, removing the redundant computation of
        separate networks and the bounding boxes of proposal-driven
        detectors.
  \item \textbf{Order-invariant training.} Hungarian matching on mask
        IoU that assigns predictions to ground-truth instances,
        resolving the label-switching ambiguity of joint training.
  \item \textbf{Honest empirical evaluation.} On 3{,}710 manually
        annotated images spanning eight mouse behavioral tests,
        HybridMTLNet reaches a PCK@0.05 of 0.9605 and an mIoU of 0.8083,
        matching or surpassing dedicated single-task baselines while
        roughly halving the computation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Estimating Animal Visual Pose}
Locating anatomical landmarks in images has progressed from classical part-based models to deep learning. Early methods, such as the deformable part models of Yang and Ramanan \cite{yang2011pck}, used mixtures-of-parts to model body configurations and popularized the Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK) as a standard metric. Newell et al.\ \cite{newell2016stacked} reshaped the field with the Stacked Hourglass Network, which casts keypoint localization as Gaussian heatmap regression. HRNet \cite{sun2019hrnet} later refined this paradigm, maintaining high-resolution feature representations throughout the network. For biological research, open-source markerless frameworks such as DeepLabCut \cite{mathis2018deeplabcut} and SLEAP \cite{pereira2022sleap} adapted these heatmap-regression methods to laboratory-animal tracking, with SLEAP extending to multi-animal social settings through Part Affinity Fields (PAFs). However, these frameworks handle only sparse keypoints and cannot capture fine object shape or boundaries.

\subsection{Dense Instance Segmentation}
Top-down, proposal-driven architectures have long been the dominant paradigm for instance segmentation. Mask R-CNN \cite{he2017maskrcnn} pioneered the detect-then-segment approach and still sets the standard. Recent iterations, such as YOLO-Seg \cite{jocher2026yolo26}, optimize these pipelines for real-time one-stage deployment. Despite their efficiency, these frameworks depend on spatial bounding-box proposals and Region-of-Interest (RoI) pooling. The downsampling inside RoI pooling introduces large quantization errors that blur object boundaries at full resolution. To relieve these artifacts, proposal-free and box-free frameworks have emerged. In particular, SOLOv2 \cite{wang2020solov2} formulates instance segmentation as a location-category prediction problem that directly yields high-fidelity dense masks from continuous feature grids without bounding-box interventions. However, these box-free models target only mask generation and do not support extra tasks such as keypoint regression or joint pose estimation.

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning of Representations}
Multi-task learning (MTL) trains a single shared backbone across multiple objective functions to learn generalized representations \cite{caruana1997multitask}. Joint segmentation and pose estimation have been integrated into unified networks, such as Parsing R-CNN \cite{yang2019parsingrcnn} and UniPose \cite{artacho2020unipose}, for human vision analysis. However, joint optimization is often hampered by gradient conflicts, also known as task interference or negative transfer, where competing objectives prevent the shared weights from reaching the best solution for each task \cite{kendall2018multitask,standley2020tasks}. Gradient surgery techniques (e.g., PCGrad \cite{yu2020pcgrad}) address this at the optimization stage. In automated animal behavioral analysis, however, multi-task tracking has traditionally relied on modular pipelines.

Frameworks such as SIPEC \cite{marks2022sipec}, STCS \cite{tang2024stcs}, and InteBOMB \cite{zhai2025intebomb} chain together independent models---for example, Mask R-CNN for instance localization followed by separate networks for identification and pose estimation. Because these models share no internal features, they repeat feature extraction and incur high latency. Recently, unified single-network alternatives such as YOLO-SP \cite{jiang2025yolosp} and MT-SRNet \cite{liu2025mtsrnet} have been proposed, performing multi-task tracking in a single framework. However, they remain detection-based and depend on either bounding-box proposals or rotated rectangles. Moreover, these models are mostly developed for agricultural settings such as crop harvesting and livestock (e.g., pigs) in open environments, and are not well adapted to the spatial constraints and structural ambiguities of small laboratory-rodent tracking.

To overcome these limitations, HybridMTLNet is a proposal-free, box-free, multi-task network that tracks laboratory mice in a single forward pass. Our framework unifies instance segmentation and keypoint localization via a shared encoder and dynamic Hungarian matching, eliminating the redundant computational cost of modular pipelines and the quantization artifacts of proposal-based single networks.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset}
The dataset comprises 3{,}710 images collected across eight distinct
laboratory behavioral tests: the Morris water maze, dark/light box,
novel object recognition, forced swim, Y-maze, mirror box, elevated
plus maze, and tail suspension. The images contain either one or two
mice (64.4\% single-mouse and 35.6\% two-mouse configurations) and
are manually annotated with per-instance binary segmentation masks
and three skeletal keypoints per animal (the nose, shoulder, and tail
base). All annotations were completed using the Label Studio tool,
where each label underwent cross-verification by a second annotator,
resolving any inconsistencies via consensus. Since the framework
requires no bounding boxes, annotations consist of masks and keypoints
only. For experimental evaluation, the dataset is partitioned into
training (2{,}968), validation (371), and testing (371) sets according
to an 80/10/10 ratio. While the source photographs feature mixed
resolutions ($640\times360$ and $1280\times720$), all subsequent
models are trained and evaluated at a standardized spatial resolution
of $256\times256$.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, HybridMTLNet performs
instance segmentation and keypoint estimation in a single forward
pass. The network consists of three components: a shared encoder,
a segmentation decoder, and a pose head.

\noindent\textbf{Shared Encoder.}
The backbone is a ResNet-34 \cite{he2016resnet} pretrained on
ImageNet. Sharing this encoder across both tasks eliminates
redundant feature extraction. Features are extracted at four
distinct resolutions and passed to the decoder via skip
connections, preserving fine spatial detail.

\noindent\textbf{Segmentation Decoder.}
The segmentation branch is a dense per-pixel decoder
\cite{ronneberger2015unet} that progressively upsamples the
low-resolution bottleneck features via bilinear interpolation.
Skip-connected encoder features restore spatial detail at each
resolution level. The output is a 2-channel mask
($2 \times H \times W$), where each channel corresponds to one
mouse. By predicting at full $256\times256$ resolution---rather
than upsampling from a low-resolution RoI grid---the decoder
produces precise mask boundaries.

\noindent\textbf{Pose Head.}
For keypoint estimation, we use heatmap regression rather than
directly regressing $(x,y)$ coordinates via fully connected
layers, an approach that discards spatial structure. The pose
head consists of three transposed-convolution layers that
progressively upsample the shared features, followed by a
$1\times1$ convolution to produce a $6 \times H \times W$
heatmap (3 keypoints $\times$ 2 instances). Each channel
encodes the likelihood that the keypoint lies at each spatial
location; at inference, the coordinates are read as the argmax
of the corresponding channel.

\noindent The two output heads are fixed-width---a two-channel
mask and six heatmaps---so the network handles at most two
instances per image, matching our dataset.

\subsection{Loss Function}
We train HybridMTLNet with a single multi-task objective. The overall
loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}$) is the weighted sum of the
segmentation loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}$) and the pose loss
($\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}}$):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{seg}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}
              + \lambda_{\text{pose}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}}
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\text{seg}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{pose}}$ are scalar
weights controlling the relative gradient magnitudes of the two tasks.

\noindent\textbf{Segmentation Loss.}
The segmentation loss combines Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and Dice
loss \cite{milletari2016vnet} for supervision at both the pixel and
structure levels:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} +
  \left(1 - \frac{2\sum p\,g + \epsilon}{\sum p + \sum g + \epsilon}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon = 1$ is a smoothing constant. BCE penalizes
pixel-wise misclassifications between the predicted and ground-truth
masks. The Dice term measures the overall foreground overlap,
preventing the network from collapsing to an all-background
prediction---a failure mode common in cage scenes where the
background dominates.

\noindent\textbf{Pose Loss.}
The pose loss is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted
heatmap ($\hat{H}$) and the target Gaussian heatmap ($H$), averaged
over all $K = 6$ keypoint channels and the $H \times W$ pixels:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}} = \frac{1}{K H W}
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \|\hat{H}_k - H_k\|_2^2
\end{equation}
The squared penalty encourages precise keypoint localization.

\noindent\textbf{Loss Weight Balancing.}
The weights are set to $\lambda_{\text{seg}} = 1$ and
$\lambda_{\text{pose}} = 500$. The target heatmaps are highly
sparse---mostly zeros except at small Gaussian peaks---so the
magnitude of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}}$ is intrinsically two to
three orders of magnitude smaller than $\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}$.
Without this scaling, segmentation dominates the gradient and the
pose head trains poorly. Section~\ref{sec:weights} analyzes this
choice empirically.

\subsection{Order-Invariant Instance Matching}
HybridMTLNet is a box-free model (a 2-channel mask and a 6-channel heatmap), and its output channels are unordered. In one image, the network may place the left mouse in the first channel, whereas for another image it may place the right mouse in the first channel. This phenomenon is termed permutation symmetry, or label switching.

We overcome this by performing a three-step alignment at each training step. \textbf{Step 1 --- Construct a cost matrix.} Let $N_{\text{pred}}$ and $N_{\text{gt}}$ be the numbers of predicted and ground-truth instances, respectively. We construct a cost matrix $C$ of size $N_{\text{pred}} \times N_{\text{gt}}$, where each entry $C_{i,j}$ represents the dissimilarity between prediction $i$ and ground-truth instance $j$, defined as one minus their mask overlap (IoU):
\begin{equation}
  C_{i,j} = 1 - \operatorname{IoU}(\hat{S}_i, S_j)
\end{equation}
Here $\hat{S}_i$ and $S_j$ denote the predicted and ground-truth instance masks. The larger the overlap, the higher the IoU and the lower the cost. \textbf{Step 2 --- Find the best matching.} We use the Hungarian algorithm (linear sum assignment) \cite{kuhn1955hungarian} to find the one-to-one matching with the lowest total cost, which is equivalent to the largest total mask overlap. \textbf{Step 3 --- Reorder the ground truth.} Using this matching, we reorder the ground-truth masks and heatmaps to match the output channels of the model, and only then compute $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}$. This guarantees that the gradient is consistent across training steps rather than contradictory. The assignment itself is non-differentiable and detached from the computation graph, so gradients flow through the loss but not through the assignment.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}
To enhance generalization and robustness, we apply online data
augmentation during training. The following transformations are applied
randomly to each training sample with probability $p = 0.5$: horizontal
flipping, vertical flipping, random rotation within $\pm 30^\circ$, and
color jittering (brightness in $[0.7, 1.3]$ and contrast in
$[0.8, 1.2]$). To maintain cross-task consistency between the two output
heads, all geometric transformations (flipping and rotation) are applied
identically to the input image, the ground-truth instance masks, and the
skeletal keypoint coordinates. By contrast, color jittering is applied
only to the input image, ensuring that photometric changes do not
distort any spatial annotation.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
All models were implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA
GeForce RTX~5060 GPU with 8~GB of memory. For HybridMTLNet, we used
the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of $1 \times
10^{-4}$. Rather than a fixed schedule, we let the rate adapt: the
ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler halves the rate whenever the validation loss
stops improving for 5 consecutive epochs. Training ran for 100 epochs at a batch
size of 16, with every input image resized to $256 \times 256$.

For reproducibility, we forced cuDNN into deterministic mode and
reused the same data split across all runs. Because GPU training is
not perfectly deterministic, we trained every configuration with three
random seeds (42, 43, and 44) and reported the mean~$\pm$~standard
deviation. The loss-weight sweep
(Table~\ref{tab:weights}) and the training curves
(Fig.~\ref{fig:training}) show a single representative run at the
chosen 1{:}500 ratio.

The baselines follow their own standard recipes. The YOLO-based models
\cite{jocher2026yolo26} were fine-tuned for 100 epochs with their
default pipelines. Mask R-CNN \cite{he2017maskrcnn}, by contrast, was trained
with SGD for the same duration under the same data augmentation as
ours, for a fair instance segmentation comparison. For multi-animal DeepLabCut
\cite{mathis2018deeplabcut}, we ran 10{,}000 iterations in
static-image mode and omitted the downstream identity-assembly stage,
since our evaluation uses isolated, non-consecutive frames rather than
video.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
To compare multi-task and single-task designs fairly, we use two
standard metrics.

\noindent\textbf{Mean Intersection over Union (mIoU).}
For instance segmentation, it measures the per-instance IoU across
the dataset:
\begin{equation}
  \text{mIoU} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  \frac{|P_i \cap G_i|}{|P_i \cup G_i|}
\end{equation}
For fairness, we use Hungarian matching on the spatial overlap to
match predicted masks to ground-truth masks. Any unmatched
ground-truth mask is scored as IoU 0.

\noindent\textbf{Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK@0.05).}
For pose estimation, it follows the standard PCK protocol
popularized by Yang and Ramanan \cite{yang2011pck} and adopted by Mathis et al.\
(DeepLabCut) \cite{mathis2018deeplabcut}, which considers a predicted
keypoint correct if its Euclidean distance to the ground-truth
annotation is smaller than a specified threshold. The threshold in
this study was set to 5\% of the image dimension, equivalent to a
radius of 12.8 pixels at a resolution of $256 \times 256$. Keypoints
tagged as invisible or occluded (annotated at source) are excluded
from the assessment set.

\subsection{Baseline Models}
We compare HybridMTLNet with four well-known single-task networks.

\noindent\textbf{Segmentation baselines:} YOLO26m-Seg
\cite{jocher2026yolo26} and Mask R-CNN \cite{he2017maskrcnn}
(ResNet-50-FPN backbone, pretrained on COCO). These models are trained
only for instance-level segmentation.

\noindent\textbf{Pose estimation baselines:} YOLO26m-Pose
\cite{jocher2026yolo26} and multi-animal DeepLabCut (maDLC)
\cite{mathis2018deeplabcut}. These models only locate joint
coordinates.

All of these baselines are single-task. Obtaining both outputs
therefore requires running two separate models, which is far more
costly than HybridMTLNet's single forward pass.

We benchmark HybridMTLNet against four established single-task

In the instance segmentation task, HybridMTLNet attains an mIoU of
0.8083, above the strongest segmentation baseline (Mask R-CNN,
\mrcnnmiou). Because our dense per-pixel decoder predicts
at the full $256 \times 256$ resolution, it avoids the RoI-quantization
that limits boundary precision in proposal-based mask heads. Our
ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) confirms that this segmentation
quality stems from the architecture, not from multi-task learning
synergy.

For pose estimation, HybridMTLNet achieves a Percentage of Correct
Keypoints (PCK@0.05) of 0.9605, an absolute gain of 0.0733 over
YOLO26m-Pose. We include multi-animal DeepLabCut (maDLC) as a
domain-standard reference, which scores 0.4098. This figure reflects
the difficulty of our evaluation protocol rather than a weakness of
maDLC: because our test set consists of isolated, non-consecutive
frames, maDLC cannot exploit the temporal tracking and
identity-assembly stages it is designed around. We therefore treat
YOLO26m-Pose as the primary pose baseline and do not claim superiority
over maDLC in its intended video setting.

In summary, each baseline is single-task and needs a separate pipeline
per metric, whereas HybridMTLNet matches or exceeds them on both
metrics in a single forward pass.

\subsection{Loss-Weight Selection}
\label{sec:weights}
We justify the pose-loss weight \(\lambda_{\text{pose}}\) empirically by
training the joint model across a range of seg:pose ratios
(Table~\ref{tab:weights}). Segmentation mIoU is stable across all
ratios (\(0.806\)--\(0.811\)), confirming it is insensitive to the
pose weight. Pose PCK, in contrast, rises sharply with
\(\lambda_{\text{pose}}\)---from 0.751 at 1:1 to 0.962 at 1:400---then
saturates. We select \(1{:}500\) as a balanced operating point: its PCK
(0.9678) sits at the saturation ceiling while its mIoU (0.8097) stays
high. Pushing the weight to 1:1000 yields a negligible PCK gain (0.9694)
but lowers mIoU, trading segmentation for pose; we prefer 1:500 to avoid
sacrificing either task.

\subsection{Single-Task Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}
To separate the effect of multi-task learning from the architecture
itself, we run a controlled ablation. Using the same network, we
compare three settings obtained by tuning the loss weights
($\lambda_{\text{seg}}, \lambda_{\text{pose}}$):
(1) a segmentation-only network, (2) a pose-only network, and (3) our
joint multi-task setup (Joint MTL). A quantitative breakdown is shown
in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

One minor confound is worth noting: in the pose-only configuration the
segmentation head is switched off, so instance matching during
training uses heatmap distance instead of mask IoU.

\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{mIoU} & \textbf{PCK@0.05} \\

The Joint MTL configuration reaches an mIoU of $0.8083 \pm 0.0012$,
essentially identical to the $0.8089 \pm 0.0009$ of the
segmentation-only model. The difference ($-0.0006$) is well within the
seed-to-seed variation, indicating that co-training neither improves
nor harms segmentation accuracy.

For keypoint estimation, the Joint MTL variant reaches a PCK of
$0.9605 \pm 0.0027$, a drop of $0.0114$ from the $0.9719 \pm 0.0018$ of
the pose-only baseline. Because this gap is larger than the
seed-to-seed variation, it is a small but genuine case of negative
transfer, or task interference
\cite{kendall2018multitask,standley2020tasks}. Since
the shared ResNet-34 encoder must serve two geometrically different
tasks, it cannot fully reach the optimum of each one.

\noindent\textbf{Can cross-task cues recover the gap?} We also tested
whether letting the heads exchange information, not just share a
backbone, reduces this interference. We added a lightweight
stop-gradient bidirectional link: the predicted mask gates each
instance's keypoint channels, and the predicted heatmaps prime the
segmentation decoder. Pose PCK then rises from $0.9605$ to $0.9667$ with
segmentation unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), recovering about half
the gap to the pose-only ceiling. This mitigates the negative transfer
but does not remove it; closing it fully would need the heavier,
gradient-coupled fusion of joint human parsing and pose
\cite{nie2018mula,xu2018padnet}, left to future work as it conflicts
with our real-time design.

The main advantage of HybridMTLNet is therefore not multi-task accuracy
synergy but architectural unification and efficiency: a single pipeline
that preserves segmentation accuracy at a small, partly recoverable cost
in pose.

\subsection{Training Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:training} shows the training and validation curves over
100 epochs. The instance segmentation task converges quickly (training
mIoU $\ge$ 0.78 by epoch 26) and then saturates, while keypoint PCK
climbs more gradually. The ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler decays the
learning rate at epoch 90 when the validation loss stops improving; the
smaller steps then push validation PCK to its peak of 0.9595 at epoch
97 (a validation peak; the result tables report test-set scores). Small
train--validation gaps (0.035 mIoU, 0.019 PCK) indicate good generalization.

\subsection{Inference Efficiency}
HybridMTLNet's efficiency comes directly from sharing one encoder. We
compare it against a modular two-model baseline built from two
single-task copies of the same architecture, which isolates the benefit
of the shared encoder (Table~\ref{tab:efficiency}). Running both tasks
in a single pass, HybridMTLNet uses only 28.7\,M parameters and
28.8\,GFLOPs---about half the modular pipeline's 57.4\,M / 57.5\,GFLOPs---and
reaches 111.8\,FPS on an NVIDIA RTX~5060 GPU (14.8\,FPS on CPU) versus
55.9\,FPS. As laboratory recordings typically run at 30--60\,FPS, this
comfortably supports real-time deployment in resource-constrained
settings.

\section{Conclusion}
We propose HybridMTLNet, a unified box-free multi-task learning
framework for simultaneous instance segmentation and keypoint
estimation of laboratory mice in one forward pass. This lets us
avoid bounding-box annotations, and we resolve instance-identity
assignments with dynamic Hungarian matching on mask IoU. Our approach is based on a shared ResNet-34 encoder, with a
dense U-Net-style decoder and a deconvolutional pose head.

Quantitative analysis shows that our unified model matches or exceeds
the best single-task baselines, including YOLO26 and Mask R-CNN. Our
controlled ablation further shows that this segmentation quality
derives mainly from the dense per-pixel architecture rather than
multi-task synergy: single-task segmentation accuracy is preserved
during joint training, at only a small, well-documented cost in pose
accuracy. The real value of this work is thus its
architectural unification and efficiency---halving parameters and
computational complexity while doubling throughput on off-the-shelf
hardware.

Our approach offers clear advantages, but two limitations point to
future work. First, the fixed two-instance output head should be
extended to variable, higher-density counts. Second, HybridMTLNet
processes only static frames; adding temporal tracking to exploit
cross-frame continuity would improve robustness for continuous
video-based behavioral phenotyping.
